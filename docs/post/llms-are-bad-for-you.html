LLMs are bad for you

27-08-2026

llm 

written by Evie Ciobanu

I do not use LLMs. I do not call them "AI". I often speak up against LLM.

This post is my current stance on LLMs. I am primarily exploring the topic from the perspective of someone that writes code, although a lot of the arguments apply in many other scenarios or fields.

I oppose LLMs for four main reasons:

  • ethical,
  • socio-political,
  • psychological safety, and
  • environmental.

Ethical

LLMs are trained with stolen work from the public (and private) domain. Books, websites, posts, open source code, everything people wrote for the benefit of other people is now fed as training data into LLMs, regardless of whether it's ethical or legal to do so. 1

Whether you believe patents or copyright/trademarks should be opposed or not, it's still blatantly obvious the same rules are not currently applied to companies training LLMs. A lot of specialised LLM outputs are also often direct copies of existing work.

LLMs are also not trained "passively", there's tons of underpaid labor (often from the global south that) is basically used to oversee the training of these models. 2 3

Social/Political

Enshittification is the natural result of the profit motive being the driver for all services. Race to the bottom, extracting as much value as possible, and cutting costs have always been key pillars in companies (especially as they grow and distance themselves from any humanity and towards spreadsheet/capital-centric entities). This did not start with LLMs, but LLMs have definitely been supercharging and accelerating this trend.

Taylorism (ridiculously rebranded as "scientific management") is the framework which primarily focuses on turning workers into replaceable cogs that focus on monotonous or singular tasks that they are expected to do as fast and cheap as possible. It's a significant factor in work alienation, and made possible by various advances in workplace automation.

LLMs bring the prospective of introducing taylorism to white collar (and artistic-adjacent jobs). Smart workers that have unique talents are terrible for business: they are costly, have opinions, care about quality, some may even have morals.

But quality is only important in the early stages of a company or product. Once they acquired enough of the market, crushed a significant part of their competition, they are free to cut costs and LLMs are the tools that are starting to allow them to do so.

LLMs are also a tool for further displacing labour and extracting more value from workers, at everyone else's expense (which is somewhat a consequence of Taylorism). It means less jobs, worse quality, worse everything for everyone except the people owning the LLMs and companies using them.

Psychological

LLMs are deliberately built to mimmick human connection, friendships, and maximize how much time people spend using them. Companies encourage people to use more LLMs because it's "faster". This erodes human connections and deteriorates mental health and happiness. 4 [^5 5

The LLM is a powerful statistical model. And it will learn from the way you address it, the specific words you use over time with them to craft and fine-tune its responses to you in ways that feel trustworthy, familiar, and that make you want to interact with it more. It's very hard for our brains to tell the difference between it and a real human; we can't evolve as fast as our technology.

I've seen a lot of people I used to respect go into this kind of LLM psychosis, and I don't think I am smarter or better than them. I am honestly terrified to use LLMs. I can definitely see myself going into such a psychosis if I started using them (and were able to ignore all the other issues I have with them). 6 7

Environmental

I don't think I need to go too deep into this one. They use a shit-ton of water and energy, they don't care about keeping said water clean, and they're loud as fuck (disturbing the environment around them as well). 8

Bonus round: they're not even competent

They produce average code, and I want to keep learning and do better than that. I enjoy what I'm doing. If I were a musician or painter, I would be so because I enjoy creating music or paintings, not because I want to listen to my own music or look at my paintings.

There's plenty of examples of LLMs getting things wrong, breaking production, deleting databases or home directories, hallucinating answers, and generally being wrong. 9 10 11

Conclusions

I won't presume to tell anyone what to do. I will just share how I am dealing with this.

Language: I do not use the term "AI". I deliberately always use the term LLM.

Personal use: I do not touch LLMs. I write and create the things I want to create all on my own. I do not ask LLMs questions, I use search engines (with LLM overviews turned off), and I try to avoid (to the best of my knowledge) LLM-generated content.

I also actively try to avoid software tools and projects that use LLMs in any capacity, and try to gravitate towards alternatives that have a strong stance against LLM use. This is unfortunately not always possible (e.g. there's no viable alternative to the Linux kernel).

Personal relations: I try to advocate against LLM use and try to help the people around me both see my perspective (this is partly why I'm writing this blog post) and reduce their LLM use. I try, to the best of my abilities, to not be aggressive or personally attack people, especially if they're using LLMs exclusively at work in order to make a living (unless their job involves writing propaganda that advocates for LLM usage).

Professional use: I would ideally love to work for companies that have a clear anti-LLM stance. This is very hard at the moment, so for now I am settling for "at least don't force me to use one".
